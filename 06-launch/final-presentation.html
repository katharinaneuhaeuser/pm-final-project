Slide 5 · Strategy — Modules 1 & 3

We keep buying titles most of our subscribers never use

Problem hook
We pay a fortune for 15,000 titles, and more than a third of our subscribers scroll past all of them and watch nothing — the group we are most likely to lose next.
Strategic crisis · 12-month view

Our heaviest viewers stay — 58% of what they watch is already curated, so they find things. The people who have almost stopped watching leave. The real risk is the 37% in between: they still open the app two or three times a week, they still pay us, and every month we make choosing harder they slide a little closer to giving up.

The smaller services are not beating us by buying more. They are beating us by making the choice easier. If this continues, these subscribers keep paying but stop opening the app — and the people who stop opening it are the ones who cancel next.

Value proposition

For casual browsers — 37% of the base, $11.40 LTV, 2.3 sessions a week — who want to start something decent in a couple of minutes without the choosing taking over the evening.

We will bring Spotlight to the home screen: a small hand-picked selection of films and series in place of the infinite scroll, curated for accessibility rather than connoisseurship.

Because Spotlight-exposed casual browsers churn 14 points lower — about $7,100 a year per 1,000 subscribers in revenue we are choosing not to collect.

The job they are hiring Spotlight to do

Let my evening start without me having to work for it.

37%of the base
−14ptschurn if exposed
$7.1k/yr per 1,000 subs
The data-backed hypothesis

Based on an 18% Spotlight reach alongside pilot cohorts retaining 12–15 points better at Month 1, I believe that moving the curated rail into the prime slot for casual browsers will raise the share with at least one 30+ minute session a week from 11% to 14% within six weeks, while total weekly plays per subscriber does not fall.

Slide 6 · Research — Module 2

The pain is real. It just has a cheap escape.

The workaround — and why it is unusual

Their workaround is memory: a mental shortlist of four titles they already know, used in place of the product's own discovery. Some leave for a second screen instead.

These viewers want something new and do not find it. But giving up and re-watching removes the pain immediately, at no cost — so it never builds into a complaint or a demand for something better. It gets relieved rather than solved. A bad workaround makes the case for us. A workaround that works does not.

Persona · The Resigned Browser

A regular subscriber who opens StreamLine two or three times a week and has quietly stopped expecting to find anything new.

PRD highlights · Must-haves for A1

  • Title and thumbnail on every card

  • Card → existing title detail page
  • played_from_rail on every play event

Slide 8 · Validation — Module 5

Does the prime slot reach the other 82%?

The pilot already proves curation keeps people watching. This tests the one thing it cannot: whether putting those picks at the top of the home screen reaches people who never went looking. A 6-week, 50/50 user-randomised test on casual browsers only.

Control (A)

The home screen as it stands. Trending rows first; "Because you watched" returning near-duplicate franchise titles; no curated surface above the fold.

Variant (B) — exactly one change

Identical, with the Spotlight rail inserted as the first row. 5–7 titles, hiding entirely below 5 eligible rather than rendering short. First-run onboarding animation plus the persistent subline. No curator notes in either arm — this tests hand-picking alone.

Stated limitations

One feature, three mechanisms: curation, prime placement, and declared authorship. A positive result cannot separate them. Doing so needs a four-arm design this sprint cannot fund — stated up front rather than left for a reviewer to find.

The baseline is blended. 11% is measured, but for the whole mobile base. The week-2 control-arm look cuts it by segment; the sample stays provisional until it does.

ParameterDecision
Primary metric% of casual browsers with at least one 30+ minute session per week — measured per user, matching the randomisation unit
Baseline11%, measured — down from 19% six months ago
MDE+3 pts (11% → 14%) — about a third of the lost ground; below it the rail does not pay for its curators
Sample / arm1,907 users · 50/50 · 6 weeks · p < 0.05 two-sided
Guardrail 1Total weekly plays per subscriber — not more than 5% below control
Guardrail 2Month-1 retention — not more than 2 pts below control
Shipping criteria — committed before launch
SHIP

≥ +3 pts at p < 0.05, with the CI's lower bound also ≥ 3, and both guardrails intact.

ITERATE

Significant but the interval's floor falls below 3 pts. First iteration is the curator's note — the Should-have this test excluded.

INVESTIGATE

Primary rises but a guardrail breaks, or segments contradict — e.g. sessions up overall, down on TV. Cuts by device, tenure and segment are fixed in advance.

KILL

Not significant, or negative. A positive non-significant result is a kill — the test was powered to detect the floor we set.

Read date fixed at week 6. One pre-registered week-2 look, control arm only, to cut the baseline by segment. No efficacy read, no stopping.

Bad signal to watch for

High rail impressions with flat card opens. The rail is being scrolled past — it reads as another promotional row rather than something a person chose. The fix is attribution and copy, not more titles.

Subtler: 30-minute sessions up while Month-1 retention is flat or falling — longer evenings, but fewer people coming back. That is what guardrail 2 catches.

This plan is conditional

The experiment is designed, not run. If it kills the feature there is no launch — the curation pipeline stops and the finding becomes the deliverable. We can still cancel all of this. That is why the test runs first.

Slide 10 · Story — Individual insights

The thing I got wrong was who this was for

Friction · the wrong user

I started with the loyal viewer — the person who knows the product and has worked out their own way around it. The data refused it: power users' churn response to Spotlight came back N/A, low churn anyway. You cannot win back a group that has not gone. That meant rewriting Module 1 well after Module 1 was finished.

Friction · the call I made against the data

Wanderers showed a bigger churn response — 22 points against 14. On paper, the better bet. I did not follow it. It is harder to change a wanderer's behaviour than to stop a casual browser becoming one — and that comes from my own e-commerce experience, not this case's data. I wrote it up as a judgement, not a result: it gives up $168 a month per 1,000 subscribers for a prevention benefit I cannot size.

Friction · evidence I built on anyway

Everything proving the problem is behavioural. Most of what pointed at curation as the answer is attitudinal — people saying what they would like. I noticed in the M1 self-review and carried on. The M3 pilot data rescued it, and I had not gone looking for that.

Aha moment

Phrase the problem so that a child could understand it.

My instructor's feedback, and the most useful thing I took from the project. It works as a test: every time I could not say something simply, either I did not understand it yet, or the argument had a hole the complicated wording was covering. Simplifying did not water the analysis down — it made the weak points obvious. I have already started applying it at work, and my team has noticed the difference.

Key takeaways · what I take forward
  • Read all the data before committing to a metric. Two of three snapshots sat unread for a fortnight. Both changed the answer.
  • Get behavioural evidence for the solution, not only the problem. That is the point to stop and go looking, not to note the weakness and move on.
  • Back my own judgement, and say when I am using it. The frameworks give the structure; the call is still mine to make.
  • Use AI, but not in the driver's seat. Fast at drafting and analysis. Poor at judgement, poor at plain speech.
