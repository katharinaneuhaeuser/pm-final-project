Slide 5 · Strategy — Modules 1 & 3

The library became a tax on the people paying for it

Problem hook
We're spending blockbuster money on a library only our most committed viewers can use, while the 37% of subscribers who open with no title in mind pay for it in scrolling and leave having watched nothing — our largest pool of revenue, and the one most likely to go.
Strategic crisis · 12-month view

Power users stay; at 58% curated consumption they already find what they want. Wanderers leave. The risk is the 37% in between — regular viewers who drift toward wanderer behaviour every month we make choosing harder. The specialists aren't beating us by buying more; they're beating us by removing the burden we keep adding.

Value proposition

For casual browsers — 37% of the base, $11.40 LTV, 2.3 sessions a week — we will launch Spotlight: a small, human-programmed selection of films and series replacing the infinite scroll, curated for accessibility rather than connoisseurship.

We're not adding more content. We're adding a point of view.

37%of the base
−14ptschurn if exposed
$7.1k/yr per 1,000 subs
The data-backed hypothesis

Based on casual browsers taking 44% of consumption from trending rows and only 31% from curated, at 2.3 sessions a week against 4.8 for power users, I believe that closing the effort-versus-payoff gap in discovery will result in those viewers starting titles they haven't seen rather than cycling known ones or leaving with nothing played — measured by a +2 pt lift in new-title play rate across all sessions opened, so abandonment counts as a failure. Protecting total weekly plays per subscriber. Decided at six weeks, with a 90-day churn read before scaling.

Slide 6 · Research — Module 2

We're not competing with inconvenience. We're competing with comfort.

The workaround — and why it's unusual

Their manual workaround is memory: a mental shortlist of four titles they already know, used in place of the product's own discovery. Some leave for a second screen instead.

What makes this different from the textbook version is that the workaround costs them nothing. Re-watching is instant and never disappoints, so nobody is forced into it — they chose it, and they're content. That makes the status quo far harder to displace than a clumsy spreadsheet would be.

Persona · The Resigned Browser

A regular subscriber who opens StreamLine two or three times a week and has quietly stopped expecting to find anything new.

Slide 8 · Validation — Module 5

Do casual browsers use a curated surface, or have they stopped looking?

Every document from Module 1 onward assumes the first. Nothing yet proves it. This is a 6-week, 50/50 user-randomised test designed to answer it before any further investment.

Control (A)

The home screen as it stands. Trending rows first; "Because you watched" returning near-duplicate franchise titles; no curated surface anywhere.

Variant (B) — exactly one change

Identical, with the Spotlight rail inserted as the first row. 5–7 titles, hiding entirely below 5 eligible rather than rendering short. First-run onboarding animation. No curator notes in either arm — this tests hand-picking alone.

Stated limitation

One feature, three mechanisms: curation, prime placement, and declared authorship. A positive result can't separate them. Doing so needs a four-arm design this sprint can't fund — said here rather than discovered by a reviewer.

ParameterDecision
Primary metricNew-title play rate — share of all sessions opened ending in a play new to that viewer
Baseline18% — assumed, not measured. Confirmed in weeks 1–2
MDE+2 pts (18% → 20%). Set by cost: below this the rail doesn't pay for its curators
Sample / arm6,039 users · 50/50 · 6 weeks · p < 0.05 two-sided
GuardrailTotal weekly plays per subscriber — not more than 5% below control
2nd guardrailEarly-abandon rate, all plays — ceiling +5 pts vs control
Shipping criteria — committed before launch
SHIP

≥ +2 pts at p < 0.05, and both guardrails intact.

ITERATE

Significant but below 2 pts. First iteration is the curator's note — the Should-have this test excluded.

KILL

Not significant, negative, or a guardrail breaks. A positive non-significant result is noise, not a weak signal.

Read date fixed at week 6. One pre-registered week-2 look, control arm only, to confirm the baseline. No efficacy read, no stopping.

Slide 9 · Launch — Module 6

Released in Phase 1. Launched in Phase 2. Six weeks apart, deliberately.

Goal

Engagement — not awareness. There's no awareness problem and no conversion event; the gap is between opening the app and using what's in it.

Audience

Casual browsers, 37% of base. Secondary: Wanderers, read separately for their larger churn response (−22 pts).

Bad signal to watch for

High rail impressions with flat card opens. The rail is being scrolled past — it reads as another promotional row rather than something a person chose. The fix is attribution and copy, not more titles.

This plan is conditional

The experiment is designed, not run. If it kills the feature there is no launch — the curation pipeline stops and the finding is the deliverable instead. A launch plan that can't be cancelled by its own evidence is a commitment with a schedule attached.

Slide 10 · Story — Individual insights

Drafted from the project record — confirm before submitting

The thing I got wrong was who this was for

Friction · the pivot

I started where the brief points — high-intent cinephiles, the viewers "who value quality over volume." Then the segment data said their churn response to Spotlight was N/A, low churn anyway. You cannot win back a segment that isn't leaving. Rewriting Module 1 in Module 4 was uncomfortable and correct.

Aha · two exits, not one

I had written the misery as though giving up always ends in a re-watch. It doesn't — people close the app with nothing played, and that is how a casual browser becomes a wanderer. It broke the metric, not just the wording: an abandoned session was falling out of the denominator, so the failure the metric existed to detect was invisible to it.

Aha · the prototype audits the PRD

Building it exposed a Must-have that depended on a Should-have. The Must list couldn't be satisfied by the Must list. No amount of re-reading the document would have found that; only trying to build it did.

Biggest takeaway

The status quo isn't inconvenience, it's contentment. A workaround that costs the user nothing is far harder to displace than a clumsy one — and most frameworks assume the opposite. That reframed the whole competitive case: we're not beating a messy process, we're beating a comfortable habit.

What I'd do next
  • Baseline the metric before anything else. 18% is assumed; every downstream number depends on it.
  • Instrument card opens, not impressions. Without it the named bad signal is undetectable.
  • Test avoidance versus appetite. "Discovering feels like a chore, so I don't" is opting out, not asking to be guided — and the whole initiative rests on which one it is.
