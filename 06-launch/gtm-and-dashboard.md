# GTM Strategy & Success Dashboard

- **Module 6 · ★ Deliverable 6** · Scenario: StreamLine Spotlight (B2C)
- Becomes the **GTM Strategy & Success Dashboard** slide. Sequenced around the experiment in [`experimentation-plan.md`](../05-experimentation/experimentation-plan.md)

## Go-to-market summary

A **Medium-tier, owned-channel launch** of the Spotlight Curated Rail to existing casual browsers, sequenced around the M5 experiment rather than ahead of it.

The goal is **engagement, not awareness**. Spotlight is an in-app surface for people who already subscribe and already open the app 2.3 times a week — the gap is entirely between opening it and using what's in it.

The feature introduces itself. A first-run onboarding animation, built into the rail rather than into the launch, says who chose the titles and how often they change — which is why no communication is needed to explain what the rail is. That leaves three genuine channels, two owned and one earned: the weekly Spotlight Digest Email, the app-store listing, and curator-led editorial the curators can share themselves. No paid media, deliberately: Spotlight does nothing for someone who is not already a subscriber.

The plan runs in four phases, and the experiment is Phase 1. The rail is *released* to 50% of casual browsers for six weeks with every outbound channel dark; the launch moment — full rollout, the digest email, the app-store update and the editorial — follows only a met ship criterion.

## What we are amplifying

A launch plan multiplies what already exists. It cannot create product-market fit, and no amount of channel work saves a product people do not want — Google Glass had working technology and no fit, while ChatGPT needed no launch plan at all because the fit was obvious.

So the first question is what this plan has to work with. The answer is unusually good: **Spotlight already works.** The cohorts that have had it since December stay 12–15 points better at Month 1, and the gap is still growing. People who find it use it and keep watching.

What we have is not a product problem. It is a distribution problem — 82% of visitors never reach the thing that works. That is exactly the situation where a launch plan earns its keep, and it is why this plan is mostly about placement and reach rather than persuasion.

## Launch status — this plan is conditional

**What the M5 experiment told us: nothing yet.** It is designed, not run. Everything from Phase 2 onward depends on a met ship criterion at the week-6 read — sessions reaching 30+ minutes up by at least 3 percentage points at p<0.05, with both guardrails intact.

**What the pilot already told us.** Spotlight has run since the December cohort and those cohorts retain 12–15 points better at Month 1. The curation is not the open question. Reach is: only 18% of visitors ever get to it, and this launch exists to change that.

**If the experiment kills the feature, there is no launch.** The curation pipeline stops, the editorial headcount goes to a better bet, the digest email is never sent, and the finding becomes the deliverable instead. A launch plan that its own evidence cannot cancel is not really a plan.

This is why the plan is sequenced the way it is, and why nothing is spent outside the product until the read.

## Launch plan

### Step 0 · Feature

**Spotlight Curated Rail** — 5–7 hand-picked films and series pinned to the top of the home screen, chosen by editors rather than the algorithm, introducing itself with a one-time onboarding animation.

### Step 1 · Goal & audience

**Goal: Engagement** — deepen adoption.

**Why this goal.** Spotlight is an in-app surface for people who already subscribe and already open the app — 2.3 times a week — and simply discover nothing when they do. There is no awareness problem in the market sense and no conversion event; the whole gap sits between opening the app and using what is in it. Judged on awareness this launch could look successful while changing nothing, and the metrics that actually matter — new-title play rate, then churn — are adoption metrics. Brand authority on discovery is a second-order benefit this launch *earns* rather than buys.

**Target audience.**

*Primary:* Casual browsers, "The Resigned Browser" — 37% of the base, $11.40 monthly LTV, 2.3 sessions per week, 44% trending consumption. They open the app with no title in mind and leave having re-watched something known, or having played nothing at all.

*Secondary:* Wanderers — 41% of the base, 1.1 sessions per week, 61% trending consumption. They receive the same rail and the same messaging, with no tailoring, because the surface is universal. They are named as a secondary audience because the segment data gives them the largest churn response of any group (−22 points against −14 for casual browsers), so their results are read separately even though the launch is not designed for them.

**Why they are secondary and not primary**, given that larger number. The three segments are one decay path, not three populations: LTV falls $14.20 → $11.40 → $8.40 as sessions fall 4.8 → 2.3 → 1.1. Wanderers are late-decay — a −22 point improvement there measures a problem already suffered, after $5.80 of monthly value has already been lost. Preserving a casual browser protects $11.40 and prevents the fall, so they never enter that bucket at a lower value. And the competitive logic points the same way: specialists do not take viewers who still open the app two or three times a week, they take the ones who have stopped expecting anything from it. Casual browsers are still contestable.

*Out of scope:* Lapsed subscribers. UXR-04 cannot be reached by an in-app rail, and win-back is not this launch's job.

### Step 2 · Launch tier

**M — Targeted, focused comms.**

**Justification.** S was the serious alternative and is worth answering directly. The rail is the first row above the fold, so nobody needs telling it exists; the product's whole thesis is subtraction, which sits badly with a campaign; and if the rail needed explaining it would already have failed, since the promise is that a person can judge a pick in seconds.

One thing breaks S, and it is not about communication at all.

**The second exit.** Casual browsers abandon in two ways — a re-watch, or closing the app with nothing played — and an in-app-only launch is structurally incapable of reaching the viewer who has stopped opening the app. That is a coverage gap in the mechanism, not a preference about outreach, and no amount of in-app polish closes it. Reaching that viewer requires something outbound, and S permits none.

Attribution was briefly a second argument for M, and it no longer is: the first-run onboarding animation moved into the feature itself, where it belongs. That is the right outcome — a product that has to be explained by marketing is a product with a design problem — but it means the tier now rests on the second exit alone, and should be defended on that basis rather than on two reasons where only one survives.

Reach is effectively the whole base, since a home-screen change is visible to everyone, but the people it is *for* are one segment addressable in channels we already own. Revenue impact is roughly $7,100 per 1,000 subscribers per year in retained revenue — meaningful, not press-release scale. Press and events would spend budget reaching people an in-app rail cannot help.

**Is this a launch or a release?** Both — six weeks apart. The rail is *released* in Phase 1: real users, production, measured, and needing no go-to-market at all, because the feature introduces itself through its onboarding animation. It becomes a *launch* in Phase 2, when the digest email, the app-store listing and curator editorial all open at once. Keeping them apart is what makes the experiment readable: no outbound channel can be targeted to a single arm, so a launch timed to the release would have destroyed the evidence the launch depends on.

**The tier is not constant across phases.** During Phase 1 the plan runs at effectively S-tier, because outreach would contaminate the experiment; the M-tier activity — digest email, app-store listing, curator editorial — all lands in Phase 2 after a met ship criterion. The tier reported here describes the complete launch, not the test window.

### Step 3 · Channels

**First, what is not a channel.** The rail introduces itself: a first-run onboarding animation, the first time a viewer sees it, says who chose the titles and how often they change. That is **product, not communication** — it ships inside the feature, it is part of the tested variant, and it is the reason no launch comms are needed to explain what the rail is. A comms treatment could not have done this job, because nothing outbound can be targeted to one experiment arm; it would have had to wait until after the read, leaving the thing being tested unexplained for six weeks.

That leaves three genuine channels, none of which can run before the launch moment in Phase 2:

**1 · Owned — The Spotlight Digest Email (A6).** Weekly, three picks, linking straight into the rail. Already in the M4 Now lane. It is the only mechanism that reaches the second exit — the viewer who stopped opening the app — and therefore the only channel that addresses a failure mode no in-app surface can reach. Held dark through Phase 1 to protect attribution.

**2 · Owned — App-store listing and release notes.** Updated screenshots and copy showing the rail. The only channel that reaches someone at the moment they are deciding whether to re-open the app at all. Cannot be targeted by arm, so it waits for Phase 2 — a control-arm subscriber seeing screenshots of a feature they do not have is both confusing and a contamination risk.

**3 · Earned — Curator-led editorial.** The curators are named people with followable taste; their weekly selections are publishable as short editorial pieces and shareable by the curators themselves. The only channel that touches the M1 strategic crisis — authority on discovery — at no media cost. Public by nature, so it waits for Phase 2, and the curators are briefed not to publish during the test window.

**No paid channel, deliberately.** Paid social would reach prospects, and Spotlight is useless to someone who is not already a subscriber. Spending media budget on an engagement feature for existing users is the clearest way to make an engagement launch read as a failed awareness campaign.

**The rule underneath all of this:** any channel that cannot be targeted by experiment arm cannot run during the test. In-app passes, because it is served per user. The app-store listing and earned media both fail, which is why no channel here opens before Phase 2.

### Step 3b · Enablement & assets

- **The first-run onboarding animation** — built into the rail, not produced as a launch asset. Copy, motion design, and the reduced-motion fallback.
- **Support brief**, covering the four minimums: what Spotlight is and who it is for; how the rail works, including why it is short and why it hides when there are too few eligible titles; **what is changing and what is not** — nothing was removed, every existing row is still there one position lower; and the common objections — "where did my rows go", "why are there only six", "why can't I filter it", "why isn't it personalised to me". That last one matters most, because the honest answer is that personalisation is the thing it exists to escape.
- **Curators briefed not to publish** until the week-6 read. Earned media cannot be targeted by arm, and one enthusiastic post mid-test contaminates everything.
- **Editorial operating brief** — the real enablement need: selection rules (5–7 titles weekly, films and series both, exclude broadly-watched titles at selection time since per-viewer exclusion is a Should-have), publication cadence, and who signs off.
- **Four weeks of picks banked before launch.** A curation pipeline that runs dry in week three kills the feature more effectively than any metric.
- Digest email template.
- Updated app-store screenshots showing the rail.

### How AI covers the gap between no budget and no marketing team

This launch has no media spend and borrows design and lifecycle time from existing capacity. That is only workable because the asset production does not have to sit in a queue.

| Where | What AI does | What we still own |
|---|---|---|
| **Assets and copy** | Drafts the digest email template, the app-store copy, the Support brief and the objection answers. First versions in an afternoon rather than a sprint. | The claims. Every number in the copy comes from the brief, not from a model. |
| **Design** | Produces directionally-right layouts for the rail and the email, so the designer starts from something close rather than a blank page. | The onboarding animation. Motion and the reduced-motion fallback are real design decisions. |
| **Automation** | Runs the weekly digest send and flags when the curation queue drops below four weeks of banked picks. | Deciding what happens when it does. |
| **Analytics** | Watches the dashboard during the test and flags anomalies fast enough to act while it is still running. | The read itself, at week 6, against criteria fixed before launch. |

**What it does not cover.** Three things in this plan came from judgement a model would not have produced, and it is worth being clear about which:

- **Choosing casual browsers over Wanderers.** The data ticked Wanderers and so did the first analysis. The call to protect people before they slide rather than rescue them afterwards is a strategic bet, not an output.
- **Reading the pilot correctly.** Spotlight already existing changes what this launch is for. Nothing surfaced that; it came from reading all three snapshots properly.
- **The curator relationships.** Editors with taste people follow are hired and briefed by a person. That is the whole product.

**Timeline.**

**Release and launch do not coincide here, deliberately.** The rail is released in Phase 1 — real users, production, measured. Nothing is *launched* until Phase 2, because no outbound channel can be targeted to one experiment arm. Letting a formal launch wait six weeks does not delay shipping anything of value.

**Phase 0 · Weeks −2 to 0 — Readiness.** Bank four weeks of picks. Brief Support, including common objections. Brief the curators not to publish until the read. Build the onboarding animation into the rail, and the email template. Digest email built but dark; app-store assets prepared but **not submitted**.

**Phase 1 · Weeks 1–6 — Beta / early access.** The release. Rail live to 50% of casual browsers, onboarding animation included as part of the variant. **No outbound communication of any kind** — digest email withheld, app-store listing unchanged, curators silent. Week-2 control-arm baseline check. Read at week 6, not before.
*Track: rail engagement rate, new-title play rate, time to first play, both guardrails.*

**Phase 2 · Week 7 — The launch moment.** On a met ship criterion: rail to 100% of subscribers, digest email launches, app-store assets submitted, curator editorial syndication begins. The first week anything is said outside the product.
*Track: activation among subscribers meeting the rail for the first time, and whether rail engagement holds outside the tested segment.*

**Phase 3 · Weeks 8–20 — Post-launch adoption.** Converting a launch moment into a habit. Digest email running weekly, curation pipeline steady, editorial cadence established. The 90-day churn read lands here.
*Track: adoption velocity, digest-originated sessions, sessions per week per segment, 90-day churn.*

### Step 5 · Success metrics

Matched to the engagement goal:

1. **Spotlight reach** — share of casual-browser sessions that open the curated selection. 18% today, target ≥35% by week 6. *Feature-level. Answers: are the other 82% reaching it now?* This is the number the whole launch exists to move, and it moves first.
2. **Time to first play from the rail** — median under 120 seconds. *Feature-level. Answers: did it make choosing cheap?* The persona's own standard is "a couple of minutes", and the primary metric is blind to cost — someone who scrolls for eighteen minutes and then plays something new counts as a success while the misery is intact.
3. **Casual browsers with at least one 30+ minute session per week** — the M5 primary. 11% baseline, down from 19%; target 14%. *Product-level. Answers: did the evening actually happen?* The case's own critical signal, and the number this initiative is judged on.
4. **Casual-browser sessions per week** — 2.3 today, target 2.8. *Product-level. Answers: did it change the habit, or only the session?* The behaviour change that precedes churn reduction, and the reason the 90-day read is worth waiting for.

**Bad signal to watch for.** High rail impressions with flat card opens. That means the rail is being scrolled past — it reads as another promotional row rather than as something a person chose. The fix is attribution and copy, not more titles; adding titles to a row nobody looks at makes it worse. A second, subtler bad signal: new-title play rate up while early-abandon rate is also up, which means we persuaded people to start things they did not want. That is what the second guardrail exists to catch.

**Most likely post-launch decision: iterate.**

This is a different decision from the week-6 ship/kill call, and it is worth keeping them apart. The experiment answers whether the rail worked under controlled conditions for one segment. The post-launch decision is driven by real market behaviour across the whole base over months, and it is read in Phase 3.

*Trigger for iterate:* rail engagement holds among casual browsers but falls off sharply among Power Users and Wanderers, or digest-originated sessions stay flat. Both say the mechanism works for the segment it was designed for and not beyond it. The adjustment is one variable — most likely the curator's note, the Should-have deliberately excluded from the test.

*Double-down* if rail engagement holds across all three segments and 90-day churn moves in the predicted direction: expand curation headcount, ship curator profiles (A7), build the second rail.

*Pivot* if engagement is strong but churn does not move at 90 days. That would mean the rail changed what people watch without changing whether they stay — a positioning problem, not a product one, and the honest response is to ask why rather than to add features.

*Deprioritize* if rail engagement decays through Phase 3 after a Phase 2 spike. A launch spike that does not become a habit is the clearest signal that curation was novelty, and the editorial headcount should go to a better bet.

**Where the 35% comes from.** Spotlight reaches 18% of visitors today and retains those people 12–15 points better at Month 1. Roughly doubling reach is the ambition the prime slot should deliver — a surface above the fold ought to be met by most of the people who open the app, not by a fifth of them.

It is also the floor the primary metric needs. To move 30-minute sessions by 3 points through a surface that converts a fraction of the people who see it, reach has to rise substantially; 18% cannot produce it arithmetically. If reach stalls near 18%, the primary target is unreachable regardless of how good the curation is, and we will know by week two rather than week six.

## Why this plan is launch-ready — and where it isn't

The audience follows from the goal rather than the other way round: an engagement goal points at existing subscribers who open the app and discover nothing, and that is exactly who the rail is built for. The tier is honestly sized, and answers the cheaper alternative rather than asserting itself. Every channel reaches the audience where it already is, and the absence of a paid channel is argued rather than overlooked. Every activity has a named owner, the single budget ask is surfaced at the top of the table rather than buried, and the metrics match the goal — with a bad signal the PM can act on and a pre-committed trigger for the most likely decision. Most importantly, the launch is sequenced around the experiment: nothing ships broadly until the week-6 read.

Two things a reviewer should know before trusting it.

**Rail engagement rate needs instrumentation that does not exist yet.** It requires card *opens*, not impressions, and the M4 Must list only committed to `played_from_rail` on play events. Impressions alone would hide the precise failure named as the bad signal — a rail that is seen and ignored. This is a dependency on engineering, not a reporting detail.

**The editorial headcount has no number against it.** It is correctly identified as the only real cost and as the thing the MDE was set against, but this plan does not size it. A rail that runs dry in week three fails more decisively than any metric, so the curation pipeline needs a staffing answer before Phase 1 begins, not after.
