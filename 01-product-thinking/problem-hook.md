# Problem Hook & Value Proposition, Module 1

- **Scenario:** StreamLine Spotlight (B2C) · **Likely risk:** Value

## Strategic crisis

If we do nothing for 12 months, we lose the subscribers in the middle — the ones we could most easily have kept.

Our heaviest viewers stay. They always would: 58% of what they watch is already curated, so they find things. The people who have almost stopped watching leave. The real risk is the 37% in between. They still open the app two or three times a week, they still pay us, and every month we make choosing harder they slide a little closer to giving up.

Meanwhile we keep buying more titles. That makes the library more expensive and harder to use for 78% of our subscribers at the same time.

The smaller services are not beating us by buying more than us. They are beating us by making the choice easier. If this continues we end up paying a fortune to be the service people keep paying for and stop opening.

## Moment of misery

They open StreamLine with no title in mind, scroll the trending rows for twenty minutes, and close the app without pressing play.

Discovery starts to feel like work, so they stop attempting it — falling back to the same four comfort shows rather than face the catalogue again, or abandoning the evening here altogether and doing something else.

They know what kind of evening they want — "quiet Sunday", "something my book club would like" — and there is no way to ask for it.

Asked what would actually help, they don't ask for more content. They ask someone to just tell them what's good tonight.

**The workaround, and why it is unusual.** Their workaround is memory: a mental shortlist of four titles they already know, used in place of the product's own discovery. Some leave for a second screen instead.

The pain is real — they want something new and do not find it. What makes this case different is that the pain has a cheap escape. Re-watching removes it immediately and at no cost, so it never builds into a complaint or a demand for something better. Nobody is forced into the workaround; they choose it, and it works.

That is harder to displace than a clumsy spreadsheet. A bad workaround makes the case for us. A workaround that works does not.

## Problem hook

> We pay a fortune for 15,000 titles, and more than a third of our subscribers scroll past all of them and watch nothing — the group we are most likely to lose next.

*Expanded, for context rather than for the deck:* We've spent a decade buying a library that only our most committed viewers can actually use. For the 37% of subscribers who open StreamLine two or three times a week with no title in mind, 15,000 options isn't choice — it's a tax paid in scrolling, and they pay it by settling for what's trending, re-watching something they've already seen, or closing the app having watched nothing at all. They are our single largest pool of revenue and the segment most likely to leave, and we are spending blockbuster money to make their evenings worse.

## Value proposition

For **casual browsers** — the 37% of subscribers who open StreamLine two or three times a week with no title in mind. They want to start something decent in a couple of minutes without the choosing taking over the evening. Today they settle for whatever is trending, go back to a show they already know, or close the app without watching anything.

They are worth more to us than any other group: about $4,220 a month for every 1,000 subscribers. They are also worth more than they currently are — each one sits $2.80 a month below what our heaviest viewers are worth, and our own data shows they respond to curation.

**The job they are hiring Spotlight to do:** *let my evening start without me having to work for it.* Not "find me the perfect film", and not "show me more" — remove the labour from the decision, so that choosing stops competing with watching.

**We will bring StreamLine Spotlight to the home screen** — the curated space already piloted inside the app, surfaced where people actually are instead of somewhere they have to go and find. A small, human-programmed selection replaces the infinite scroll: a handful of titles at a time, chosen and explained by people whose taste you can follow, with context for why each is worth watching now. Spotlight programmes **films and series alike**, and it curates for **accessibility rather than connoisseurship** — the promise is a confident, well-reasoned pick for tonight, not a cinephile syllabus. We're not adding more content; we're adding a point of view. Spotlight turns 15,000 titles from a scrolling problem into a recommendation we've earned the right to make.

**Because** our own segment data shows Spotlight-exposed casual browsers churning 14 points lower than unexposed ones. On a 1,000-subscriber base that is roughly $590 a month, about $7,100 a year, in retained revenue we are currently choosing not to collect. Every quarter of delay also moves more of this segment down into wanderer behaviour, where value per head falls to $8.40 and win-back costs more than retention ever would.

## Cold-read self-review

**Strongest:** the inversion. "The library is a benefit only to viewers with the appetite to mine it; for everyone else it's a tax" reframes our core asset as a liability for the majority, and it's defensible from our own mix data — 58% curated consumption for power users against 31% for casuals. It makes the case structural rather than a UX complaint.

**Weakest:** the assumption that casual browsers *want* curation. The evidence is thinner than it looks, and it is thin in a specific, nameable way. Every piece of evidence establishing the **problem** is behavioural — twenty-minute scrolls ending in nothing, four comfort shows, 2.3 sessions a week, 44% trending consumption. Every piece suggesting the **solution** is attitudinal. UXR-03 describes avoidance, not appetite: "discovering anything new feels like a chore, so I don't" is someone opting out, not someone asking to be guided. The clearest demand signals, UXR-09 and UXR-12, are stated preferences from older participants, and sit close to the hypothetical question type that invites people to describe a product they would like rather than report what they do. Nobody has been observed using a curated surface, because none exists.

> **Correction, 2026-10-03.** The last sentence is wrong, and the weakness is smaller than this review claims. The Module 3 cohort data shows Spotlight piloted from the December cohort at 18% reach, with those cohorts retaining 12–15 points better at Month 1. Behavioural evidence that people use a curated surface and stay longer does exist. The cold read is left as written because it records the judgement made at the time, on the evidence available then — but the risk it names should no longer be carried at full weight.

**Sharpen, in order:**

1. Separate avoidance from appetite. Find or run evidence that casual browsers will *use* a curated surface, not merely that they have given up on the current one. This is the assumption the whole initiative rests on.
2. Defend the distinctiveness. Curating for accessibility across films and series is the right call for this persona, but it costs us the sharp edge the specialists have. If Spotlight is "good things, explained," we need one sentence on why that is a defensible position rather than a better home screen — attributable curators with followable taste is the likeliest answer.
3. Name the power-user question before someone else does. We are deliberately not building for our highest-LTV segment. The answer is that they do not churn and already consume curated content — but it needs saying out loud.

**On the brief's own wording.** The scenario asks us to "win back segments that value quality over volume," which points at cinephiles and lapsed high-value viewers. We are targeting casual browsers instead, and the reason is in the segment data rather than in preference: power users show no measurable churn response to Spotlight, because they are not leaving. You cannot win back a segment that has not gone. What casual browsers offer is the same curation thesis applied where it can actually move a number — and "values quality over volume" describes them accurately too, once you stop reading it as a synonym for connoisseurship. Someone who wants one good evening rather than fifteen thousand options values quality over volume exactly as the brief means it.
